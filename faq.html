Getting started

Bring photos of the current space, images you admire, a list of frustrations and wishes, and any plans or measurements you have. Think about how many people use the room and at what times of day.

You do not need a finished plan. A good consultation is a conversation that helps you shape one.

Sort your list into three groups: essentials, strong preferences and nice-to-haves. Ask yourself which problems you want solved first, such as storage, layout, lighting or appearance.

When trade-offs arise later, these priorities make decisions quicker and calmer.

Helpful information includes the room type and approximate size, photos, what you want to change, any must-keep elements, your preferred timeframe and a realistic budget range you are comfortable discussing.

Planning & materials

Materials influence structure, lead times, maintenance, installation methods and cost. Heavy stone may require reinforced cabinetry, for example, and some finishes need to be ordered well ahead.

Deciding major materials early helps avoid redesigns midway through a project.

Plan where belongings will be stored, set up a temporary kitchen or washing arrangement if needed, protect furniture, and think about access, pets and household routines.

Ask what level of dust, noise and limited access to expect so there are no surprises.

Budget & timelines

Scope is what is being changed; budget is how much you are comfortable investing. Changing layout, plumbing or structure generally has different implications than updating surfaces and fittings.

This site does not quote prices. A real contractor would provide figures specific to your home after understanding the project.

Common factors include the size and complexity of the scope, how quickly decisions are made, material lead times, permits or approvals where required, hidden conditions discovered during work, and trade availability.

No schedule is promised here. Timelines should always be discussed for your specific project.

Kitchens vs. bathrooms

Kitchens involve many appliances, work zones and storage types, and are often shared social spaces. Decisions revolve around workflow, ventilation, surfaces and cabinetry.

Bathrooms are smaller and more technical. Waterproofing, drainage, ventilation, slip resistance and fixture positions leave less room for improvisation.
